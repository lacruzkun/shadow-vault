
## Multi-vendor e-commerce as a platform for small businesses

Understanding the problem addressed by this study requires first situating it within the development of electronic marketplaces. E-commerce has expanded beyond the traditional model in which a single retailer presents and sells its own catalogue to customers, towards electronic marketplaces in which independent buyers and sellers interact through a common digital intermediary (Bakos, 1991; Wang et al., 2010). Research on electronic marketplaces commonly distinguishes the marketplace from an ordinary online shop by its role in coordinating participants and exchanges rather than simply presenting the products of one organisation (Wang & Archer, 2007; Wang et al., 2008). Multi-sided platform theory similarly conceptualises such systems around the interaction of distinct participant groups, typically buyers and sellers, and emphasises the organisational and economic consequences of coordinating those groups through a shared platform (Hagiu & Wright, 2015).

The platform model creates requirements that are less prominent in conventional single-vendor e-commerce (Hagiu & Wright, 2015; Wang & Archer, 2007). Research on e-marketplaces identifies the platform, sellers, buyers, products, payment mechanisms and service operations as interrelated success factors rather than independent features (Prihastomo et al., 2018). A marketplace must therefore preserve the interests and identities of participating sellers while reducing the search and transaction costs experienced by buyers (Bakos, 1991; Hagiu & Wright, 2015). Bakos (1991), for example, argues that electronic markets can create value partly by reducing buyers’ search costs and improving access to information about prices and product offerings. This helps explain why the marketplace model can be particularly useful to small businesses: participation in a shared platform can provide access to a broader customer base without requiring each business to establish and attract traffic to an independent online store. Established platforms such as Amazon and Taobao, discussed below, show this in practice (Alibaba Group, 2020; Bezos, 2019).

The multi-vendor structure also creates a governance problem (Boudreau & Hagiu, 2009; Tiwana et al., 2010). Unlike a single retailer, a marketplace operator does not exercise complete organisational control over every seller participating on the platform (Boudreau & Hagiu, 2009). Recent research consequently distinguishes formal mechanisms such as monitoring from informal mechanisms such as procedural fairness when examining how platform firms manage sellers (Sen et al., 2023). Similarly, Danani et al. (2025) demonstrate that platform owners face choices concerning the degree and form of seller control available to them. The literature therefore suggests that a functioning marketplace requires mechanisms through which sellers can be admitted, monitored and, where necessary, restricted in their participation (Boudreau & Hagiu, 2009; Danani et al., 2025; Sen et al., 2023).

Governance is nevertheless not simply a matter of imposing restrictions. Sen et al. (2023) show that monitoring and procedural fairness can affect seller performance, indicating that governance operates through both control and the perceived legitimacy of platform decisions. This creates a tension between maintaining platform standards and preserving productive relationships with independent sellers (Boudreau & Hagiu, 2009; Sen et al., 2023). The literature therefore provides a rationale for explicit seller governance while also cautioning against treating technical enforcement as sufficient in itself (Danani et al., 2025; Sen et al., 2023). Within the context of a small-business marketplace, this is particularly relevant because sellers may differ significantly in their levels of technical experience, organisational capacity and familiarity with online commerce (Awa et al., 2015).

## Established e-commerce marketplaces and their relevance to small businesses

The marketplace model is not only a theoretical category; it describes the operating structure of several of the largest commercial platforms. Amazon provides a clear illustration. In his 2018 letter to shareholders, Bezos (2019) reported that independent third-party sellers accounted for 58% of the company’s physical gross merchandise sales, compared with 3% in 1999, and noted that most of these sellers were small and medium-sized businesses. He attributed part of this growth to the selling tools provided to independent sellers, together with Fulfilment by Amazon and the Prime programme (Bezos, 2019). The Amazon case therefore suggests that the value of a marketplace to small sellers depends not only on access to buyers but also on the quality of the tools available for managing listings, stock and orders. This is consistent with the emphasis placed in the present study on a vendor dashboard and integrated inventory management.

Alibaba’s Taobao Marketplace follows a comparable logic. Alibaba Group (2020) describes its merchants as primarily individuals and small businesses who can create storefronts and listings free of charge, and notes that a search on Taobao returns listings from both Taobao Marketplace and Tmall merchants. Search is therefore the mechanism through which many independent sellers become visible to buyers. eBay and MercadoLibre, which Hortaçsu et al. (2009) describe as large online auction sites, represent an earlier generation of platforms that connect individual buyers and sellers directly.

In Nigeria, Jumia, which describes itself as the leading pan-African e-commerce platform, is a prominent example of a multi-vendor platform operating at scale. It describes its platform as a marketplace connecting more than 70,000 sellers with customers, supported by a logistics network and a proprietary payment service, JumiaPay (Jumia Technologies AG, 2025). Jumia therefore combines marketplace, delivery and payment functions within a single organisation, a degree of integration that lies beyond the resources of a small-business platform. This contrast supports the bounded scope of the present study, which concentrates on marketplace, inventory and payment functions and assumes that physical delivery is handled by external providers.

These platforms point to three recurring features: tools through which independent sellers manage their offerings, search that makes those sellers visible to buyers, and supporting services such as payment and fulfilment. Their scale and ancillary services nevertheless differ greatly from what a small-business marketplace can provide, and much of the available evidence consists of corporate disclosures rather than independent analysis. Material from large platforms is therefore used here to illustrate established practice, not as evidence of causal effects.

## Location, proximity and vendor discovery

In a marketplace where many vendors may list similar products, search is the main way in which buyers find what they need. Bakos (1991) argued that electronic markets create value partly by lowering search costs, and the usefulness of search depends on the information displayed alongside each result. Price is the obvious example; the physical location of the seller is another.

Evidence from large online marketplaces suggests that location continues to matter even where transactions are conducted online. Analysing transactions on eBay and MercadoLibre, Hortaçsu et al. (2009) found that distance remained an important deterrent to trade between geographically separated buyers and sellers, although less so than in offline commerce, and that buyers showed a strong tendency to trade with counterparties in the same city. The authors attribute this home bias mainly to cultural factors and to the possibility of direct contract enforcement if something goes wrong (Hortaçsu et al., 2009). For a Nigerian small-business marketplace in which physical delivery is arranged outside the platform, these findings suggest that buyers may value being able to identify vendors who are geographically close to them, although this is an inference from the literature and not a finding of the cited study.

Established platforms already expose seller location in their search and listing features. Etsy allows sellers to set a shop location so that their listings appear in local searches, and lets buyers filter results by shop location (Etsy, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Jiji, a Nigerian online classifieds marketplace, similarly allows sellers to indicate their location so that listings can reach buyers in a particular city or region (Jiji Nigeria, n.d.). In both cases the location is declared by the seller in advance and attached to listings, rather than being computed at the point of search.

This pattern is directly relevant to the platform proposed in this study. Vendors supply their business location when they create their profiles; when a buyer searches for a product, the results can then present listings from different vendors together with each product’s price and the vendor’s location, allowing the buyer to select a nearby vendor or continue browsing until one is found. Because location is declared by the vendor, the administrator’s approval of vendor registrations provides a point at which it can be reviewed. This design keeps proximity under the buyer’s control and avoids the need for automated distance calculation or route planning, which would add technical complexity beyond the scope of the study.

The pattern also affects how participants are modelled. In a marketplace for small businesses, a vendor may also be a buyer: a business that sells one category of goods may need to purchase others from fellow vendors. The platform therefore needs to allow an approved vendor to use the buyer-facing search, cart and checkout in addition to the vendor dashboard, which has implications for the role-based access control discussed in the next section.

## Trust, security and role-based access in online marketplaces

The value created by an electronic marketplace depends ultimately on whether participants are willing to trust the system sufficiently to transact through it (Gefen et al., 2003; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). The absence of physical interaction, combined with concerns about privacy, security and seller behaviour, makes trust a recurring theme in e-commerce research (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Soleimani, 2022). Soleimani’s (2022) synthesising review of 129 studies shows that trust in e-commerce is multidimensional and can involve the platform, sellers, technology, institutional environment, privacy and perceived risk. Trust consequently cannot be understood as a characteristic of a particular seller alone; it is also shaped by the environment in which the transaction takes place (Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Soleimani, 2022). Earlier research likewise positioned trust alongside perceived usefulness and ease of use as an important determinant of consumers’ willingness to shop online (Gefen et al., 2003).

The relationship between trust and security has been reinforced by more recent evidence. Handoyo (2024), through a meta-analysis of e-commerce research, reports significant relationships involving trust, perceived risk, perceived security and purchasing decisions, with perceived risk moderating the relationship between trust and purchase behaviour. This suggests that security mechanisms have both technical and behavioural significance: they protect transactions while also contributing to users’ perception that online participation is sufficiently safe (Handoyo, 2024; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). Research focused on electronic marketplaces similarly identifies trust as one of the important dimensions influencing successful marketplace implementation (Prihastomo et al., 2018).

These concerns are especially relevant to the Nigerian environment. Esho and Verhoef (2021), studying online shopping in Nigeria, found that both trust in vendors and perceived website quality positively affect consumers’ purchase intentions, while website quality carried greater relative weight. This finding is important because it indicates that trust cannot be separated entirely from the quality of the digital environment through which the transaction occurs. A Nigerian review by Igwe et al. (2020) likewise identifies security, privacy, perceived risk and trust as significant considerations in e-commerce adoption and argues that user-friendly design and appropriate security measures are necessary to strengthen confidence in electronic commerce. Nigerian evidence therefore supports an integrated view in which security, usability and trust reinforce one another rather than operating as separate concerns (Esho & Verhoef, 2021; Igwe et al., 2020).

One established technical response to the multi-user security problem is role-based access control (RBAC). Instead of defining permissions for every individual user, RBAC associates permissible actions with organisational roles. NIST describes RBAC as a model in which access permissions are linked to roles rather than directly to individual identities, while earlier NIST work demonstrated its applicability to web environments and its potential to reduce the administrative complexity of access control in large networked applications (Barkley et al., 1998; Jansen, 1998). For multi-vendor commerce, this approach is particularly appropriate because the platform contains recognisably different responsibilities: customers interact with purchasing functions, vendors manage their own commercial activities, and administrators exercise supervisory authority. Role separation therefore reflects the organisational structure of the marketplace as well as its security requirements (Barkley et al., 1998; Jansen, 1998). The same logic accommodates account holders who perform more than one function: because a vendor in a small-business marketplace may also purchase from other vendors, the vendor role can be extended with the purchasing permissions associated with the customer role without granting any administrative authority.

Security, however, extends beyond authentication and authorisation. Web applications are exposed to threats such as cross-site request forgery, cross-site scripting and injection attacks, meaning that secure application design requires controls at several levels rather than a single login mechanism (OWASP Foundation, n.d.-a, n.d.-b, n.d.-c). Secure development guidance consequently emphasises measures such as server-side validation, protection against forged requests and safe handling of application data (OWASP Foundation, n.d.-a, n.d.-b, n.d.-c). The significance of this layered approach is consistent with the trust literature: consumers do not directly observe internal security mechanisms, but weaknesses in those mechanisms can increase perceived or actual risk, while their presence contributes to the reliability of the service environment (Handoyo, 2024; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004).

## Electronic payments and transactional integrity

For an e-commerce platform, the payment stage provides the point at which trust and convenience must be translated into a verifiable financial transaction. Electronic payment research in Nigeria provides strong support for the importance of this area. Oyelami et al. (2020), using survey data from Lagos, found significant relationships between e-payment adoption and factors including convenience, security and safety, trust and social influence. Their results also indicate that financial infrastructure and internet availability influence the adoption environment. Payment capability is therefore not simply an optional extension to e-commerce functionality; it can influence whether consumers are prepared to complete transactions electronically (Oyelami et al., 2020).

The literature on payment adoption, however, is more heavily focused on behavioural and socioeconomic determinants than on the internal integrity of the software processes that follow a payment event (see, for example, Oyelami et al., 2020). This creates an important distinction. From a consumer perspective, payment must be convenient, trusted and secure (Oyelami et al., 2020). From a system perspective, a payment must also correspond correctly to an order and its subsequent business consequences (Flutterwave, n.d.; Fowler, 2002). Once a successful transaction is established, the system may need to update the order state, record the payment, adjust available stock and communicate the result to relevant participants (Fowler, 2002). These operations create dependencies among several system states and therefore raise issues of consistency and transactional control that are not captured fully by adoption models (Haerder & Reuter, 1983).

The literature also supports server-side verification as a principle for security-sensitive transactions. Security guidance for web applications consistently cautions against trusting data supplied by clients for decisions that have financial or authorisation consequences (OWASP Foundation, n.d.-d). For payment systems, this implies that a customer-facing success state should not by itself be treated as proof that a transaction has been successfully completed (Flutterwave, n.d.; OWASP Foundation, n.d.-d). Verification needs to take place through trusted server-side communication with the payment provider or equivalent mechanism (Flutterwave, n.d.). In this respect, established security principles complement the behavioural literature on payment trust: consumers require confidence that the financial process is safe, while the application requires authoritative evidence that the transaction actually occurred (Flutterwave, n.d.; OWASP Foundation, n.d.-d).

## Inventory management and order fulfilment

The importance of inventory management follows directly from the relationship between product availability and the customer’s online purchasing experience. Nguyen et al. (2018), in a systematic review of online consumer behaviour and order fulfilment, identify stock-outs and other fulfilment outcomes as factors that can influence purchase and repurchase behaviour. Their review is significant because it connects operational activities that are sometimes treated as back-office concerns with consumer responses that directly affect the success of online retailing. Accurate inventory information is therefore not only an internal business requirement but also part of the customer experience (DeHoratius & Raman, 2008; Nguyen et al., 2018).

The issue is particularly relevant to small businesses. Kabuye et al. (2020), studying 304 small businesses in Uganda, found significant associations between inventory management and financial performance. Their findings support the view that inventory practices have implications beyond day-to-day stock control and can contribute to broader business outcomes. Narkhede and Rajhans (2022) similarly note that SME adoption of inventory-management strategies can be constrained by the availability of cost-effective and easy-to-use techniques. Their study demonstrates the potential of integrated inventory approaches to reduce costs and stock-outs, but it also highlights the complexity that can arise when inventory optimisation involves forecasting, clustering, lot-sizing and replenishment policies (Narkhede & Rajhans, 2022).

This creates a useful distinction between **inventory visibility and control** on the one hand and **inventory optimisation** on the other. A marketplace serving small businesses does not necessarily need to solve the full forecasting and replenishment problem in order to deliver value (Kabuye et al., 2020; Narkhede & Rajhans, 2022). At a minimum, sellers need reliable information about available quantities, customers should not be permitted to purchase quantities that cannot be supplied, and stock should change consistently when transactions are completed (DeHoratius & Raman, 2008; Haerder & Reuter, 1983). These requirements correspond to the more basic end of inventory-management capability while remaining closely related to the stock-out concerns identified in fulfilment research.

## Usability, responsive access and e-commerce website quality

A technically capable e-commerce platform can still struggle to attract or retain users if the interaction experience is difficult, inconsistent or poorly adapted to the devices through which customers access it (Davis, 1989; Gefen et al., 2003). This is especially relevant in the present context because Nigerian research has found a strong relationship between perceived website quality and online purchase intention, with website quality carrying greater relative weight than trust in vendors in the study conducted by Esho and Verhoef (2021). Website quality is therefore not merely a presentation concern; it forms part of the conditions under which consumers assess an online service (Esho & Verhoef, 2021; Igwe et al., 2020).

The growth of mobile access further complicates the usability problem (DataReportal, 2026). Responsive web design attempts to adapt the presentation of a website to different screen sizes and orientations without requiring a separate application for every device category (Marcotte, 2010). Kumar (2022) argues that responsive e-commerce interfaces need specific usability attention because mobile interaction differs from desktop interaction, and proposes a mobile-first guideline derived from the practices of established responsive e-commerce websites. The study's findings support the idea that usability should be considered systematically across device contexts rather than assumed to follow automatically from a desktop-oriented design (Kumar, 2022).

The broader literature also suggests that perceived ease of use can contribute to trust and acceptance. Gefen et al. (2003) integrated trust with the Technology Acceptance Model and found that usefulness, ease of use and trust are interconnected in online shopping behaviour. This is particularly relevant to a marketplace containing several user types because complexity affects not only customers but also vendors who must manage products, stock and transactions (Davis, 1989; Gefen et al., 2003). Excessive friction in either the buying or selling process can therefore undermine the value of the marketplace even when the underlying functionality is available (Narkhede & Rajhans, 2022).

## Architectural modularity and maintainability

The integration of marketplace governance, authentication, payments, inventory and communication also raises the question of how such a system should be organised internally. Software architecture literature has long argued for separating presentation concerns, business logic and data access so that complex business applications remain understandable and maintainable (Fowler, 2002; Parnas, 1972). Fowler (2002) identifies layering, domain logic patterns and the Service Layer as established approaches for enterprise applications, with the Service Layer defining a boundary around business operations and coordinating actions that may involve multiple resources. These principles are particularly relevant where one business event affects several areas of the system (Fowler, 2002).

The literature also connects modular architecture with maintainability and quality attributes. More recent empirical work examining layered architecture argues that architectural structure and interface design can affect maintainability and usability, reinforcing the importance of selecting architecture according to desired quality attributes rather than purely according to implementation convenience (Yousaf et al., 2025). This supports an architectural view in which maintainability should be considered from the beginning of system design, particularly for applications expected to evolve as business requirements change (Parnas, 1972; Yousaf et al., 2025).

## Dispute management, notifications and platform-mediated trust

Trust within a marketplace is tested not only when transactions proceed successfully but also when expectations are not met (Katsh et al., 2000; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004). Because independent sellers and buyers interact through a common platform, disagreements concerning products, payments or orders can become a platform-level concern (Katsh & Rabinovich-Einy, 2017; Katsh et al., 2000). Online dispute resolution (ODR) research highlights the importance of trust in mechanisms through which electronic-commerce disputes are handled. Abedi et al. (2019) identified knowledge, expectations of fairness and ethical standards as important elements in establishing trust in ODR systems. The implication is that dispute handling requires more than a technical channel through which complaints can be submitted; participants must also perceive the process as understandable and fair (Abedi et al., 2019).

Notifications provide a related function by keeping participants informed about important changes in system state (Nguyen et al., 2018). Research on online fulfilment shows that post-purchase processes contribute to consumer behaviour, meaning that the customer experience does not end when payment has been completed (Nguyen et al., 2018). Notifications can therefore be understood as part of the communication infrastructure that supports order progress, payment confirmation and other consequential events (Nguyen et al., 2018; Rao et al., 2011). In a multi-vendor environment, the importance of timely information is increased because several parties may need to respond to changes in the same overall transaction (Rao et al., 2011).

## Synthesis of the literature and research gap

Taken together, the literature demonstrates that a multi-vendor e-commerce platform is a socio-technical system rather than a simple online product catalogue (Baxter & Sommerville, 2011). Electronic marketplace research identifies the coordination of buyers and sellers as the defining characteristic of the model and highlights the importance of platform governance, trust, payment, service operations and other interconnected factors (Wang et al., 2008; Prihastomo et al., 2018). Trust research shows that perceived security, website quality, perceived risk and confidence in sellers all influence the willingness to transact online (Soleimani, 2022; Handoyo, 2024). Nigerian studies reinforce these relationships within the local environment, particularly with respect to website quality, trust, security and electronic-payment adoption (Esho & Verhoef, 2021; Igwe et al., 2020; Oyelami et al., 2020). Evidence from large commercial platforms shows how these concerns appear in practice: Amazon and Taobao have grown through tools and storefronts for independent, largely small, sellers, Jumia combines marketplace, logistics and payment services in Nigeria, and Etsy and Jiji attach seller location to listings (Alibaba Group, 2020; Bezos, 2019; Etsy, n.d.-a; Jiji Nigeria, n.d.; Jumia Technologies AG, 2025). Research on the geography of online trade further indicates that buyers continue to favour nearby counterparties (Hortaçsu et al., 2009), which supports presenting vendor location during product search.

A similar pattern appears in software architecture. Established architectural research provides techniques for separating business concerns and managing complex transactions, but architecture itself does not solve marketplace governance, trust or fulfilment problems. Instead, it provides an organisational foundation through which those requirements can be implemented coherently (Fowler, 2002). The literature therefore points towards integration as a central challenge (Baxter & Sommerville, 2011; Tiwana et al., 2010). Research often focuses on one dimension—consumer trust, platform governance, inventory, payments or architecture—while an operational marketplace must make these dimensions function together (Tiwana et al., 2010).

The research gap addressed by this study can consequently be framed as an **integration and contextualisation gap**. The project does not claim that multi-vendor commerce, electronic payment, inventory management, role-based access control or responsive web design are individually novel. Rather, it brings established concepts together within a bounded web platform intended for small businesses operating in Nigeria. The significance of this approach lies in connecting functions that are often discussed separately: platform governance with trust, payment with inventory, vendor location with product discovery, fulfilment with customer communication, and architectural modularity with the management of these interdependent processes (Baxter & Sommerville, 2011; Tiwana et al., 2010).

The study also maintains a deliberately bounded scope. Advanced analytics, warehouse management, automated vendor payouts, loyalty programmes, native mobile applications and physical delivery logistics are outside its focus. Vendor location is likewise treated as declared profile information displayed in search results, and automated distance calculation and route planning are not pursued. These boundaries distinguish core marketplace capability from the more sophisticated functions of large commercial ecosystems, such as the combined marketplace, logistics and payment operations of Jumia (Jumia Technologies AG, 2025), and keep the research centred on the principal technical and organisational requirements of a small-business marketplace (Awa et al., 2015).

## Conclusion

The literature reviewed establishes that successful multi-vendor e-commerce depends on the coordinated treatment of several interrelated concerns. Electronic marketplace studies explain the distinctive role of a platform in bringing independent buyers and sellers together and highlight the importance of governance and platform-level coordination (Boudreau & Hagiu, 2009; Hagiu & Wright, 2015; Wang et al., 2008). Trust research demonstrates that security, perceived risk, website quality and seller confidence influence online purchasing decisions, while Nigerian studies show the particular relevance of these factors in the local market (Esho & Verhoef, 2021; Handoyo, 2024; Igwe et al., 2020; Pavlou & Gefen, 2004; Soleimani, 2022). Electronic-payment research further demonstrates the importance of trust and security to payment adoption, while fulfilment and inventory research links stock availability and operational reliability to consumer behaviour and small-business performance (DeHoratius & Raman, 2008; Kabuye et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2018; Oyelami et al., 2020). Finally, software architecture literature provides established methods for structuring complex business applications so that interconnected processes remain manageable and maintainable (Fowler, 2002; Parnas, 1972; Yousaf et al., 2025). Evidence from established platforms such as Amazon, Taobao, Jumia and Etsy shows how these concerns are handled in practice, including seller tools, searchable listings and declared seller location, while research on the geography of online trade indicates that buyers continue to value proximity to sellers (Alibaba Group, 2020; Bezos, 2019; Etsy, n.d.-b; Hortaçsu et al., 2009; Jumia Technologies AG, 2025).
