## Multi-vendor e-commerce as a platform for small businesses

Understanding what the project sets out to achieve requires first situating it within the wider shift in how online commerce is structured. The emergence of electronic marketplaces has changed e-commerce from a model in which a single retailer controls the product catalogue into platform-based arrangements in which multiple independent sellers can interact with a shared customer base [Citation needed]. This distinction is particularly important for the present project because its purpose is not merely to digitise an individual shop, but to provide a common marketplace through which multiple small businesses can reach customers. Multi-sided platform research conceptualises such systems around the coordination of separate participant groups, typically buyers and sellers, while recognising the organisational and economic trade-offs created by allowing independent providers to participate in a shared environment (Hagiu & Wright, 2015).

This platform perspective creates requirements that are less prominent in conventional single-vendor e-commerce. A single-shop system can often treat its products, inventory and customer relationships as belonging to one organisational entity [citation needed]. By contrast, a multi-vendor environment must preserve the distinction between the platform, vendors and customers while still presenting customers with a unified shopping experience [citation needed]. Research on electronic marketplaces similarly emphasises that platform owners have only limited direct authority over independent sellers and therefore require mechanisms for controlling and aligning seller behaviour with platform objectives (Danani et al., 2025).

This issue directly informs the project's decision to introduce **vendor approval, suspension, rejection and status history** rather than allowing every registered vendor to immediately publish products. The implementation therefore reflects a governance structure in which vendor access is treated as a controlled privilege. The presence of dedicated vendor profiles, approval states and administrative actions provides a mechanism for separating ordinary account registration from participation as a marketplace seller. This is consistent with research suggesting that marketplace governance requires deliberate seller-selection and control mechanisms rather than assuming that open participation alone will produce a reliable platform (Danani et al., 2025). The implementation also provides an administrative route for resolving customer-vendor disputes, reinforcing the platform's role as an intermediary rather than merely a passive hosting service.

The literature, however, also reveals a limitation in treating marketplace governance purely as a technical control problem. Sen et al. (2023) find that monitoring and procedural fairness can both improve seller performance, while the wider platform-governance literature indicates that inappropriate or inconsistent control can create conflict and reduce trust. Governance must therefore balance enforcement with perceived fairness. The project's manual administrative approval, vendor status history and dispute process address part of this requirement by creating explicit points at which decisions can be made and recorded. Nevertheless, the implementation remains intentionally bounded: it does not attempt sophisticated seller reputation scoring, automated fraud detection, incentive systems or algorithmic governance. This represents a suitable boundary for a final-year project focused on enabling small businesses to participate in a shared marketplace rather than reproducing the extensive governance infrastructure of large commercial platforms.

## Trust, security and role-based access in online marketplaces

Governance mechanisms such as vendor approval only address part of what makes a marketplace viable; the other, arguably more decisive, factor is whether customers and vendors trust the platform enough to transact on it at all [citation needed]. Trust is one of the most persistent themes in e-commerce research because online transactions remove many forms of direct interpersonal reassurance available in physical commerce. Soleimani's (2021) systematic review of 129 e-commerce studies demonstrates that trust is a multidimensional phenomenon involving relationships among customers, sellers and platforms, rather than a single property of a website. The review associates trust with factors including website characteristics, security, reputation, perceived risk and institutional conditions. This perspective is particularly relevant to a multi-vendor marketplace because customers must place trust not only in individual sellers but also in the platform that mediates their interactions.

Earlier work likewise establishes that perceived usefulness and ease of use interact with trust in shaping online shopping behaviour. Gefen et al. (2003) found that trust is important alongside the Technology Acceptance Model's perceived usefulness and ease-of-use constructs, and identified safety mechanisms and a familiar, usable interface as contributors to trust. More recent meta-analytic evidence continues to support the importance of trust, perceived risk and perceived security in online purchasing decisions, with perceived risk affecting the relationship between trust and purchase behaviour (Handoyo, 2024).

These issues are particularly significant in the Nigerian context. Research focused specifically on Nigeria has linked perceived risk to lower trust in online shopping, while perceived usefulness is associated with actual use (George et al., 2015). Esho and Verhoef (2021) further found that both trust in vendors and perceived website quality positively affect online purchase intentions among Nigerian consumers, with website quality carrying greater weight in their study. A broader Nigerian review similarly concludes that security, privacy and trust remain important conditions for e-commerce adoption and recommends secure and user-friendly platform design.

These findings provide a strong basis for the project's combination of security controls and interface design. At the application level, passwords are hashed rather than stored in plain text; authenticated routes use Flask-Login; customer, vendor and administrator access is separated through role-oriented decorators; and vendor-specific operations check ownership before allowing a resource to be modified. This reflects the logic of **role-based access control (RBAC)**, in which permissions are associated with organisational roles rather than individually assigned to every user. NIST's foundational RBAC work argues that role-based permissions simplify access-control administration by grouping privileges according to organisational responsibilities (Barkley et al., 1998; Jansen, 1998).

The implementation's separation of CUSTOMER, VENDOR and ADMIN roles is therefore more than a convenient programming abstraction. It is an architectural response to the multi-party nature of the marketplace. Customers require access to purchasing functions; vendors require access to their products, stock and orders; and administrators require broader supervisory access. The additional distinction between a registered vendor and an **approved vendor** further refines this model by treating marketplace participation as conditional rather than equivalent to ordinary authentication.

Security requirements also extend beyond login. OWASP guidance identifies CSRF, XSS and injection as significant threats to web applications and recommends mechanisms such as server-side CSRF validation, contextual output encoding and parameterised database access. The codebase uses Flask-WTF/CSRF protection, server-side form validation and SQLAlchemy database access, while Jinja templates provide framework-level output escaping. The implementation should therefore be understood as adopting established defensive practices rather than treating authentication alone as sufficient security.

However, the literature also exposes an important boundary. The existence of secure authentication and authorisation mechanisms does not automatically establish consumer trust. Trust is influenced by the total user experience, including website quality, predictability, perceived fairness and the behaviour of vendors. The project's dashboard-oriented interface, visible account separation, notifications, product information, vendor stores and responsive design can consequently be interpreted as complementary trust mechanisms rather than simply aesthetic choices.

## Electronic payments and transactional integrity

Trust in a marketplace is ultimately tested at the point of payment, where security perceptions must translate into a working, verifiable transaction. Payment functionality is another critical component of online commerce because it connects the customer-facing interface with irreversible financial transactions and downstream operational processes. Nigerian evidence demonstrates that adoption of electronic payment systems is positively associated with factors including convenience, safety and security, trust, financial inclusion and the availability of supporting infrastructure (Adu et al., 2020). In that study, security and safety were significant influences on electronic-payment adoption, reinforcing the importance of treating payment integration as both a technical and behavioural problem.

The present project addresses this issue through a payment architecture in which an order is first created in a pending-payment state and a separate payment record is associated with it. This distinction is important because order creation and payment confirmation are not necessarily simultaneous operations. The code generates a payment reference, initializes a Flutterwave transaction, and then verifies the transaction on the server before considering the payment successful. The webhook additionally checks its signature and performs server-side verification rather than trusting client-supplied payment status.

This design is consistent with the wider principle that security-sensitive actions must be validated by the server rather than inferred from untrusted client data. More broadly, the project's payment process illustrates how e-commerce functionality is dependent upon maintaining consistency across multiple transactional states. A successful payment must update the payment record, order status and vendor-order status, and must also trigger inventory changes and notifications. Consequently, payment cannot be regarded simply as an isolated user-interface feature.

The literature nevertheless exposes a gap between academic discussions of payment adoption and the practical integrity requirements of an integrated marketplace. Studies of electronic-payment adoption commonly focus on behavioural determinants such as convenience, trust and security rather than the internal consistency of payment, order and inventory states (Adu et al., 2020). The project responds to this implementation-oriented gap by connecting payment confirmation to transactional business logic. Its service layer ensures that successful payment is the event that authorises stock reduction and order progression. This is particularly important where several independent vendors may contribute items to a single customer order.

The project's use of a **mock payment pathway** alongside Flutterwave integration is also appropriate within its development and testing scope. It allows payment-dependent functionality to be exercised without requiring every development or test scenario to invoke an external financial service. At the same time, the presence of actual server-side verification means that the architecture is not conceptually dependent on trusting a simulated client response.

## Inventory management as an integrated e-commerce function

Payment confirmation does not end at the transaction itself; it also triggers changes elsewhere in the system, most notably in stock levels, which raises the closely related question of how inventory is managed. Inventory management is central to the project's identity because the system is explicitly intended to combine marketplace functionality with inventory control. Research on online retailing has established that inventory availability is directly connected to consumer behaviour. Nguyen et al. (2018), in a systematic review of online consumer behaviour and fulfilment, identify inventory management, product availability and stock-outs as important dimensions of online retailing. Their review shows that stock-outs can influence purchasing decisions and longer-term repurchase behaviour, while highlighting the fragmented nature of previous research linking operational fulfilment to consumer behaviour.

The relevance to small businesses is equally clear. Research on small businesses associates inventory management with financial performance, suggesting that effective inventory practices are not merely operational conveniences but contribute to organisational outcomes (Kabuye et al., 2020). Nigerian research has also identified inventory accuracy, stock-out frequency, turnover, forecasting and replenishment among important dimensions of inventory management in digitally enabled business environments (Ayantoyinbo et al., 2024). These findings provide direct justification for integrating inventory into the marketplace rather than treating it as a separate back-office concern.

The codebase implements this integration in several ways. Vendors can view their stock, adjust quantities and define low-stock thresholds. The customer-facing marketplace filters products according to availability, and cart operations prevent customers from adding quantities greater than available stock. More importantly, the system does not permanently reduce inventory at the moment an item is added to a cart. Stock is reduced when successful payment has been confirmed. This distinction reduces the possibility that temporary cart activity will unnecessarily remove stock from actual inventory.

The strongest technical response to the inventory problem appears in the payment service, where stock reduction is performed using an atomic database update with a condition requiring sufficient stock. This is intended to prevent overselling when competing transactions attempt to purchase the same item. The system then records an inventory transaction containing previous stock, new stock, quantity change, order reference and reason. Such transaction records provide a trace of stock movement rather than reducing inventory to a single unexplained numeric value.

Low-stock alerts extend this design from passive record keeping to basic operational decision support. The system compares new stock against a vendor-defined threshold and notifies the vendor when inventory crosses that boundary. This is conceptually consistent with research that positions inventory visibility and timely stock information as important elements of online fulfilment.

The project deliberately stops short of advanced forecasting or optimisation algorithms. Research exists on demand forecasting, replenishment policies and integrated inventory optimisation for SMEs, including approaches based on forecasting and lot-sizing models (Narkhede et al., 2022). However, such approaches introduce a level of analytical complexity outside the declared scope of the project, which explicitly excludes sophisticated analytics and enterprise-level inventory functions. The resulting system therefore occupies a useful middle ground: it automates stock recording, validation, transaction history and threshold alerts without claiming to solve the wider optimisation problem of enterprise supply chains.

## Usability, responsive access and seamless interaction

Even a system with sound governance, trustworthy security and reliable inventory tracking depends on whether customers and vendors can actually use it comfortably across devices. Security and functionality alone do not guarantee effective e-commerce adoption. Website quality and ease of use have repeatedly been connected to trust and purchase intentions, particularly in the Nigerian context [citation needed]. Responsive design is therefore a significant technical concern for the present project because the scope explicitly excludes a native mobile application while requiring effective use across mobile browsers and different screen sizes.

Research into responsive e-commerce design demonstrates that mobile users encounter usability challenges caused by differences in screen size and orientation, and that mobile-first usability practices can improve the overall user experience (Kumar, 2022). This provides a conceptual basis for the project's responsive interface rather than treating desktop presentation as the default and mobile presentation as an afterthought.

The implementation goes further than CSS responsiveness. Its JavaScript layer intercepts same-origin links and forms, retrieves the next server-rendered page through `fetch`, and replaces the document body while preserving the broader interface shell. Browser history is updated using the History API, and local interactions are re-initialised after navigation. This creates an interaction model that retains the simplicity of server-rendered Flask pages while reducing visible full-page reloads. The approach is particularly appropriate to the project's stated requirement for a fluid user experience between sections of the platform.

The implementation also uses consistent design tokens, reusable product cards, dashboard metric cards, visual focus states and reduced-motion handling. These features support a broader interpretation of usability in which accessibility and perceptual consistency are treated as quality attributes rather than decorative enhancements. The relationship between usability and trust in the e-commerce literature strengthens this interpretation: interface consistency and ease of use can affect how users perceive the reliability of an online service (Gefen et al., 2003).

Nevertheless, responsive web design remains distinct from a native mobile application. The project appropriately limits itself to browser-based responsiveness, consistent with its scope. This boundary is important because the literature on mobile commerce increasingly includes native applications, cross-channel ecosystems and device-specific functionality that cannot be assumed to apply directly to a responsive web platform. The current project instead prioritises broad accessibility through the browser while avoiding the development and maintenance costs associated with a separate application.

## Architectural modularity and maintainability

None of these customer-facing qualities, however, can be sustained without an underlying codebase capable of supporting them as the system grows. The technical organisation of the system reflects principles from enterprise application architecture in which presentation, business logic and data access are separated to improve organisation and maintainability. Fowler's enterprise application patterns identify layering and the Service Layer as recurring solutions for data-intensive business systems; the Service Layer establishes a boundary around business operations rather than allowing application interfaces to contain all business logic directly (Fowler, 2002). Similarly, layered architecture separates presentation, business and persistence responsibilities, allowing each layer to perform a more focused role.

The codebase closely reflects this principle. Flask blueprints separate major functional domains, while services encapsulate significant business operations such as order creation, payment handling, vendor state transitions, notifications and disputes. Models represent persistent domain entities and relationships, while Jinja templates provide the user-facing presentation. This structure is especially appropriate for a system whose features are interdependent: placing an order affects payments, vendor orders, inventory and notifications, while changing vendor status affects marketplace participation.

The architecture also supports testability. The supplied project contains tests covering authentication, product creation and ownership, vendor approval, cart and checkout behaviour, inventory changes after payment, and dispute creation. This is significant because the most important project requirements are not simply visual; they concern business rules and relationships among actors and records. Automated tests provide a means of verifying those rules as the system evolves.

There is, however, an important qualification concerning verification. The supplied environment did not contain the project's Python dependencies at inspection time, so the test suite could not be executed successfully in this analysis environment; the attempt failed because Flask was not installed. Thus, the presence of tests in the codebase demonstrates an intended verification strategy, but it does not constitute independent confirmation here that every test currently passes. This distinction should be preserved in the final project documentation.

## Dispute management, notifications and platform-mediated trust

A well-architected system still cannot prevent disagreements between customers and vendors from arising, which brings the discussion back to trust — this time expressed not through security controls but through how the platform mediates conflict once it occurs. A marketplace introduces disputes that do not arise in exactly the same form in a single-vendor shop because the platform must mediate relationships among participants. Research on online dispute resolution highlights the importance of fairness, expectations and confidence in systems through which digital disputes are resolved. Studies argue that trust in online dispute-resolution environments depends not simply on technological capability but also on perceptions of knowledge, fairness and ethical conduct.

The present implementation addresses this at a deliberately basic level. Customers can create disputes associated with their orders, add notes, and receive updates; administrators can review disputes, add administrative notes and change dispute status. This creates an internal communication trail rather than requiring all disagreements to be handled outside the platform.

Notifications provide the connective layer between these features. Successful payment generates customer and vendor notifications; changes in order status are communicated to the relevant customer; vendor approval decisions generate notifications; disputes generate administrator or customer updates; and inventory thresholds create vendor alerts. Rather than being an isolated messaging feature, notifications therefore operate as an information-propagation mechanism across the platform's main state transitions.

This design responds to a gap that can be observed in much e-commerce literature: trust is frequently studied as a consumer perception, while the concrete mechanisms through which a platform continuously communicates and mediates transactions receive less attention. The project's contribution is consequently practical and architectural: trust-supporting behaviour is embedded into transaction workflows through visible states, notifications and administrative intervention rather than being represented solely by a security badge or static privacy statement.

## Synthesis of the literature and research gap

Taken together, the literature suggests that an effective e-commerce marketplace cannot be reduced to a product catalogue and shopping cart. Multi-sided platform research establishes the need to coordinate independent sellers and buyers; governance research demonstrates the importance of monitoring and seller control; trust research shows that perceived security, website quality and seller reliability shape purchase behaviour; Nigerian studies reinforce the importance of trust, security, usability and electronic-payment conditions in the local context; fulfilment literature links inventory availability to purchasing and repurchasing behaviour; and software architecture literature supports modular separation of business responsibilities.

A key gap is that these areas are frequently treated independently. Research may examine e-commerce trust without implementing inventory controls, inventory studies may focus on operational optimisation without marketplace governance, and platform-governance studies may focus on seller behaviour without addressing the detailed technical state transitions through which transactions are executed. The present project responds to this fragmentation by integrating these concerns into a single system designed specifically for small businesses within the Nigerian market.

The project's contribution is therefore not the introduction of a completely novel e-commerce concept, since multi-vendor marketplaces, online payments, inventory systems and RBAC are individually established technologies and research areas. Its contribution is the **integration and contextualisation of these established approaches into a coherent, bounded platform for Nigerian small businesses**. The resulting architecture reflects the project's scope: it provides role-based marketplace governance without advanced automated platform control, inventory automation without enterprise forecasting, electronic payment without automated vendor payout, dispute management without a full external online-dispute-resolution ecosystem, and responsive web access without a native mobile application.

One implementation detail deserves explicit recognition in interpreting this contribution. Although the revised project objectives remove delivery management from the formal research objectives, the codebase contains order and vendor-order states such as dispatch, transit and delivery-related statuses, together with administrator status-management functionality. This should not be interpreted as inclusion of physical logistics within the study scope. Rather, it represents software-side recording of order states after vendor dispatch. The distinction is consistent with the stated boundary that physical delivery logistics are handled externally.

## Conclusion

The literature establishes that the design of an effective multi-vendor e-commerce platform requires simultaneous attention to marketplace governance, trust and security, electronic payments, inventory accuracy, usability and maintainable software architecture. Existing studies consistently demonstrate that buyers' willingness to transact online is influenced by trust, perceived security, website quality and convenience, while marketplace research emphasises the need to govern independent sellers and fulfilment research links product availability to consumer behaviour. The Nigerian context further strengthens the case for secure, usable and electronically enabled commerce, while architectural literature supports modular separation of presentation, business logic and persistent data.

Against this background, the supplied project can be positioned as an applied response to a practical gap in the literature: the integration of these concerns into a focused, small-business-oriented Nigerian marketplace rather than addressing each concern in isolation. Its role-based security, vendor approval model, multi-vendor ordering, server-side payment verification, atomic inventory updates, threshold-based alerts, notifications, dispute management, responsive interface and modular service architecture collectively operationalise concepts identified across the literature. At the same time, the deliberate exclusion of automated payouts, advanced analytics, warehouse management, loyalty systems and physical logistics keeps the system aligned with its stated scope. These findings provide the conceptual and technical foundation for the subsequent methodology and system-design chapter, where the chosen architecture, database structure, user roles, workflows and implementation techniques can be examined in detail.
